Why Does Linear Elasticity Need Only Two Material Constants?

From volumetric and distortional deformation to the elasticity tensor

Engineering Mechanics
Why does isotropic linear elasticity require only two independent material constants? Starting from volumetric and deviatoric strain, this article derives Hooke’s law, the fourth-order elasticity tensor, Voigt notation, and the plane-stress and plane-strain stiffness matrices.
Author

Goangseup Zi

Published

October 7, 2026

Keywords

linear elasticity, isotropic elasticity, elasticity tensor, constitutive law, Hooke’s law tensor form, bulk modulus, shear modulus, Lame constants, Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, volumetric strain, deviatoric strain, Voigt notation, plane stress, plane strain, elastic stiffness matrix

English | 한글

We have already introduced two objects that are central to continuum mechanics: the stress tensor and the strain tensor. Stress tells us about internal force, while strain tells us about local deformation.

But knowing both tensors still leaves the most important material question unanswered:

If a material deforms by a certain amount, how much stress does it develop?

This is the constitutive question. In the Z Diagram, it is the connection

\[ F \leftrightarrow \sigma \underbrace{\leftrightarrow}_{\text{constitutive law}} \varepsilon \leftrightarrow u. \]

For linear isotropic elasticity, we often jump immediately to Hooke’s law or a \(6\times6\) elastic stiffness matrix. That is convenient computationally, but it hides the mechanics.

A more physical route begins by asking what a small deformation can actually do to a material element.

There are two fundamentally different possibilities: it can change volume, or it can change shape. Once this distinction is made, the reason an isotropic linear elastic material needs only two independent elastic constants becomes almost unavoidable.

A strain tensor contains two different kinds of deformation

Consider the infinitesimal strain tensor \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). It can always be decomposed as

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{vol}} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dev}} } \]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{vol}} = \frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\mathbf I = \frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\mathbf I, \]

and

\[ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dev}} = \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} - \frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\mathbf I. \]

In index notation,

\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} + \left( \varepsilon_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} \right) } \]

with

\[ \varepsilon^{\mathrm{dev}}_{ii}=0. \]

The trace

\[ \varepsilon_v=\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon_{kk} \]

is the infinitesimal volumetric strain.

The physical distinction matters more than the algebra:

  • \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{vol}}\) changes volume without changing shape;
  • \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dev}}\) changes shape without changing volume.

Imagine first inflating a tiny rubber cube uniformly. Its shape remains a cube, but its volume changes. Now imagine shearing it into a parallelepiped while keeping its volume essentially unchanged. These are mechanically different deformation modes.

That observation is the key to the constitutive law.

Stress can be decomposed in the same way

The Cauchy stress tensor can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{vol}} + \boldsymbol{s} } \]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{vol}} = \sigma_m\mathbf I, \qquad \sigma_m=\frac{1}{3}\sigma_{kk}, \]

and the deviatoric stress is

\[ \boldsymbol{s} = \boldsymbol{\sigma} - \frac{1}{3}\sigma_{kk}\mathbf I. \]

Thus,

\[ s_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\sigma_{kk}\delta_{ij}, \qquad s_{ii}=0. \]

If pressure \(p\) is defined positive in compression,

\[ p=-\frac{1}{3}\sigma_{kk}=-\sigma_m. \]

Again there are two physical parts: a mean normal stress associated with volumetric response, and a deviatoric stress associated with distortion.

Why does isotropic elasticity need only two constants?

An isotropic material has no preferred material direction. Rotate the specimen and its constitutive response does not change.

For small-strain linear elasticity, the volumetric and deviatoric modes can therefore be related independently and linearly to their corresponding stresses:

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{vol}} =3K\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{vol}} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{s} =2G\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dev}}. } \]

Combining them,

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\sigma} = 3K\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{vol}} + 2G\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dev}}. } \]

This equation contains the physical reason for the famous result that an isotropic linear elastic solid has only two independent elastic constants.

It must answer only two independent stiffness questions:

How strongly does the material resist change of volume?

How strongly does the material resist change of shape?

The answers are the bulk modulus \(K\) and shear modulus \(G\).

Bulk modulus: resistance to volume change

For purely volumetric deformation,

\[ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_v\mathbf I. \]

Then

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}=K\varepsilon_v\mathbf I, \]

so that

\[ \boxed{K=\frac{\sigma_m}{\varepsilon_v}}. \]

Thus \(K\) measures resistance to volume change.

Shear modulus: resistance to shape change

For simple shear,

\[ \gamma_{12}=2\varepsilon_{12}, \qquad \tau_{12}=\sigma_{12}. \]

The deviatoric constitutive relation gives

\[ \sigma_{12}=2G\varepsilon_{12}, \]

or

\[ \boxed{\tau_{12}=G\gamma_{12}}. \]

Thus \(G\) measures resistance to distortion.

The two constants are not merely two numbers required to complete an equation. They describe two different ways in which a solid can resist deformation.

Hooke’s law emerges from this decomposition

Substituting

\[ \varepsilon_{ij}^{\mathrm{vol}} =\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} \]

and

\[ \varepsilon_{ij}^{\mathrm{dev}} =\varepsilon_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} \]

into the constitutive relation gives

\[ \sigma_{ij} =K\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} +2G\left( \varepsilon_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} \right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \sigma_{ij} =2G\varepsilon_{ij} +\left(K-\frac{2}{3}G\right)\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij}. \]

Define the Lamé constants

\[ \boxed{ \lambda=K-\frac{2}{3}G, \qquad \mu=G. } \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \sigma_{ij} = \lambda\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} +2\mu\varepsilon_{ij}. } \]

This is the tensor form of Hooke’s law for an isotropic linear elastic material.

The important point is the direction of the reasoning. We did not begin with \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\). We began with two physical deformation mechanisms and obtained the Lamé form from them.

Where do \(E\) and \(\nu\) fit?

In engineering, Young’s modulus \(E\) and Poisson’s ratio \(\nu\) are usually more familiar because they arise naturally from a uniaxial test.

For

\[ \sigma_{11}=\sigma, \qquad \sigma_{22}=\sigma_{33}=0, \]

we define

\[ \varepsilon_{11}=\frac{\sigma}{E}, \]

and

\[ \varepsilon_{22}=\varepsilon_{33} =-\nu\frac{\sigma}{E}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \varepsilon_v =\varepsilon_{kk} =\frac{\sigma}{E}(1-2\nu), \]

while

\[ \sigma_m=\frac{\sigma}{3}. \]

Using \(\sigma_m=K\varepsilon_v\) gives

\[ \boxed{ K=\frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)}. } \]

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{ G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}. } \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ \lambda =\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}, \qquad \mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}. } \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ (E,\nu) \Longleftrightarrow (K,G) \Longleftrightarrow (\lambda,\mu). } \]

These are not three different material models. They are three different coordinate systems, so to speak, for describing the same two-parameter isotropic elastic response.

There is also useful physical information hidden in these conversions. As \(\nu\rightarrow 0.5\), \(K\rightarrow\infty\) while \(G\) can remain finite. The material becomes increasingly resistant to volume change without necessarily becoming infinitely resistant to distortion. This is the mechanics behind near-incompressibility.

Why does a fourth-order elasticity tensor appear?

Now write

\[ \varepsilon_{kk}=\delta_{kl}\varepsilon_{kl}. \]

Then

\[ \lambda\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij} = \lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\varepsilon_{kl}. \]

Because the strain tensor is symmetric,

\[ 2\mu\varepsilon_{ij} = \mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})\varepsilon_{kl}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \sigma_{ij} = \left[ \lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}) \right]\varepsilon_{kl}. \]

Comparing this with

\[ \boxed{ \sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}, } \]

we identify

\[ \boxed{ C_{ijkl} = \lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}). } \]

The fourth-order elasticity tensor is therefore not an abstract mathematical object added to the problem. Its order follows from what it must do: it maps one second-order tensor, strain, into another second-order tensor, stress.

In compact tensor notation we may write

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathsf C:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \]

Here the double contraction removes two index pairs and leaves a second-order tensor. As always, the safest way to understand the operation is to inspect the free indices:

\[ \sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}. \]

The free indices \(i,j\) remain, so the result is a second-order tensor.

Symmetry, isotropy, and homogeneity are different ideas

These words are often placed close together, but they answer different questions.

Because

\[ \varepsilon_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ji} \]

and, in a classical continuum without couple stresses,

\[ \sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}, \]

the elasticity tensor has the minor symmetries

\[ \boxed{ C_{ijkl}=C_{jikl}=C_{ijlk}. } \]

For an elastic material derived from a strain-energy potential,

\[ \boxed{C_{ijkl}=C_{klij},} \]

which is the major symmetry. A general three-dimensional linear elastic material with these symmetries has 21 independent elastic constants.

Isotropy is a statement about direction. The constitutive response is unchanged by rotation of the material axes. That additional restriction reduces the 21 constants to two.

Homogeneity, by contrast, is a statement about position:

\[ \boxed{C_{ijkl}(\mathbf x)=C_{ijkl}.} \]

An isotropic material need not be homogeneous, and a homogeneous material need not be isotropic. A functionally graded isotropic material is an obvious example of the first case; a homogeneous unidirectional composite illustrates the second.

Why do we replace the tensor by a matrix?

Finite-element codes and engineering calculations eventually need arrays of numbers. Because stress and strain are symmetric, their six independent components can be collected into vectors.

Here we use the ordering

\[ \boxed{11,\;22,\;33,\;23,\;13,\;12.} \]

Define

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm V} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{11}\\ \sigma_{22}\\ \sigma_{33}\\ \sigma_{23}\\ \sigma_{13}\\ \sigma_{12} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm V} = \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{11}\\ \varepsilon_{22}\\ \varepsilon_{33}\\ \gamma_{23}\\ \gamma_{13}\\ \gamma_{12} \end{bmatrix}, \]

where

\[ \gamma_{23}=2\varepsilon_{23},\qquad \gamma_{13}=2\varepsilon_{13},\qquad \gamma_{12}=2\varepsilon_{12}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm V} =\mathbf D\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm V}. } \]

For isotropic linear elasticity,

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{11}\\ \sigma_{22}\\ \sigma_{33}\\ \sigma_{23}\\ \sigma_{13}\\ \sigma_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{E}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)} \begin{bmatrix} 1-\nu & \nu & \nu & 0 & 0 & 0\\ \nu & 1-\nu & \nu & 0 & 0 & 0\\ \nu & \nu & 1-\nu & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-2\nu}{2} & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-2\nu}{2} & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-2\nu}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{11}\\ \varepsilon_{22}\\ \varepsilon_{33}\\ \gamma_{23}\\ \gamma_{13}\\ \gamma_{12} \end{bmatrix}. } \]

The matrix \(\mathbf D\) is not new mechanics. It is a computational representation of the same fourth-order constitutive mapping \(\mathsf C\).

The factor of two in the engineering shear strains is important. Forgetting whether a Voigt vector uses \(\varepsilon_{12}\) or \(\gamma_{12}=2\varepsilon_{12}\) is one of the easiest ways to introduce a factor-of-two error into constitutive calculations.

Plane strain: constrain deformation

For two-dimensional work we use the in-plane ordering

\[ \boxed{11,\;22,\;12.} \]

Plane strain imposes the kinematic constraints

\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_{33}=\gamma_{23}=\gamma_{13}=0. } \]

But generally

\[ \sigma_{33}\neq0. \]

The material develops whatever out-of-plane stress is required to prevent out-of-plane strain.

The in-plane constitutive relation becomes

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{11}\\ \sigma_{22}\\ \sigma_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{E}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)} \begin{bmatrix} 1-\nu & \nu & 0\\ \nu & 1-\nu & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-2\nu}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{11}\\ \varepsilon_{22}\\ \gamma_{12} \end{bmatrix}. } \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf D_{\mathrm{plane\ strain}} = \frac{E}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)} \begin{bmatrix} 1-\nu & \nu & 0\\ \nu & 1-\nu & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-2\nu}{2} \end{bmatrix}. } \]

The out-of-plane stress is

\[ \boxed{ \sigma_{33} = \frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)} (\varepsilon_{11}+\varepsilon_{22}). } \]

This is the physical meaning of plane strain: the deformation is constrained, and stress appears to enforce that constraint.

Plane stress: constrain stress

Plane stress imposes instead

\[ \boxed{ \sigma_{33}=\sigma_{23}=\sigma_{13}=0. } \]

But generally

\[ \varepsilon_{33}\neq0. \]

From the three-dimensional constitutive law, imposing \(\sigma_{33}=0\) gives

\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_{33} =-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu} (\varepsilon_{11}+\varepsilon_{22}). } \]

The in-plane law is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{11}\\ \sigma_{22}\\ \sigma_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{E}{1-\nu^2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \nu & 0\\ \nu & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-\nu}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{11}\\ \varepsilon_{22}\\ \gamma_{12} \end{bmatrix}. } \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf D_{\mathrm{plane\ stress}} = \frac{E}{1-\nu^2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \nu & 0\\ \nu & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \dfrac{1-\nu}{2} \end{bmatrix}. } \]

Here is an important computational point: plane stress is not obtained by simply deleting the third row and column of the three-dimensional stiffness matrix.

The out-of-plane strain \(\varepsilon_{33}\) is unknown. It must first adjust so that \(\sigma_{33}=0\). Only after eliminating that unknown do we obtain the plane-stress matrix. In matrix language, the operation is a Schur complement, or constitutive static condensation.

That is why the plane-stress and plane-strain matrices look different even though both describe the same three-dimensional material.

Plane stress and plane strain are different physical assumptions

The distinction can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Plane strain: }\varepsilon_{33}=0, \quad \sigma_{33}\neq0\ \text{in general}, \\[2mm] \text{Plane stress: }\sigma_{33}=0, \quad \varepsilon_{33}\neq0\ \text{in general}. \end{array} } \]

Plane strain is a kinematic constraint. Plane stress is a stress constraint.

This distinction is more useful than memorizing two stiffness matrices. It tells us which approximation is physically appropriate for a particular structure.

Back to the Z Diagram

The stress-tensor article explained how internal force becomes stress. The strain-tensor discussion explained how deformation is represented locally. Linear elasticity now supplies the constitutive bridge between them:

\[ \boxed{ F \leftrightarrow \sigma \underbrace{\leftrightarrow}_{\text{material behavior}} \varepsilon \leftrightarrow u } \]

where the final symbol represents displacement \(\mathbf u\); more explicitly,

\[ \boxed{ F \leftrightarrow \boldsymbol{\sigma} \leftrightarrow \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \leftrightarrow \mathbf u. } \]

The constitutive relation is the part of the Z Diagram where material behavior enters mechanics. Equilibrium alone cannot determine deformation, and compatibility alone cannot determine stress. We need a material law to connect the two.

For isotropic linear elasticity, that law becomes especially transparent when we begin not with a matrix, but with a physical observation:

A small deformation can change volume or change shape. An isotropic elastic material therefore needs one stiffness for each mode.

From that simple statement come \(K\) and \(G\), the Lamé constants, \(E\) and \(\nu\), the fourth-order elasticity tensor, the Voigt stiffness matrix, and finally the plane-stress and plane-strain forms used every day in finite-element analysis.
