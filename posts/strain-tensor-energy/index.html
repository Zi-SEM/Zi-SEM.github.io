From traction work to internal strain energy

From stress to strain

In the previous article, we asked what the stress tensor really represents.

Starting from an imaginary internal cut, we found that the Cauchy stress tensor provides the local mapping

\[ \mathbf n \quad\xrightarrow{\;\boldsymbol{\sigma}\;} \quad \mathbf t^{(\mathbf n)}, \]

or

\[ \mathbf t^{(\mathbf n)} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf n. \]

The stress tensor therefore tells us how internal force is transmitted across an arbitrarily oriented surface.

But this immediately raises another question.

What deformation measure naturally pairs with stress when mechanical work is considered?

We often write the internal virtual work as

\[ \delta W_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_\Omega \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \,dV. \]

This formula is fundamental in solid mechanics and finite element analysis.

But why does the strain tensor \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) appear here?

Why not simply the displacement gradient \(\nabla\mathbf u\)?

The answer follows naturally from traction work, equilibrium, and the symmetry of the stress tensor.

Start from traction work

Consider a body occupying a region \(\Omega\) with boundary \(\partial\Omega\).

Let \(\mathbf t\) denote the traction acting on the boundary and \(\delta\mathbf u\) an admissible virtual displacement.

The virtual work done by the surface tractions is

\[ \delta W_t = \int_{\partial\Omega} \mathbf t\cdot\delta\mathbf u \,dA. \]

From Cauchy’s traction law,

\[ \mathbf t = \boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf n, \]

where \(\mathbf n\) is the outward unit normal to the boundary.

Therefore,

\[ \delta W_t = \int_{\partial\Omega} (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf n) \cdot \delta\mathbf u \,dA. \]

This is where the result of the previous stress-tensor discussion becomes the starting point of the present one.

We now want to transform this boundary work into a quantity defined throughout the volume.

From the boundary to the volume

Using indicial notation,

\[ \delta W_t = \int_{\partial\Omega} \sigma_{ij}n_j\delta u_i \,dA. \]

Applying the divergence theorem gives

\[ \delta W_t = \int_\Omega (\sigma_{ij}\delta u_i)_{,j} \,dV. \]

Expanding the derivative,

\[ \delta W_t = \int_\Omega \sigma_{ij,j}\delta u_i \,dV + \int_\Omega \sigma_{ij}\delta u_{i,j} \,dV. \]

Two terms have appeared.

The first involves the divergence of stress.

The second involves the displacement gradient.

Equilibrium tells us what to do with the first.

Equilibrium removes the body-force contribution

The local equilibrium equation is

\[ \sigma_{ij,j}+b_i=0, \]

where \(b_i\) denotes the body force per unit volume.

Thus,

\[ \sigma_{ij,j} = -b_i. \]

Substituting into the traction-work expression,

\[ \delta W_t = - \int_\Omega b_i\delta u_i \,dV + \int_\Omega \sigma_{ij}\delta u_{i,j} \,dV. \]

The total external virtual work includes both surface tractions and body forces:

\[ \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \delta W_t + \int_\Omega b_i\delta u_i \,dV. \]

The body-force terms cancel, leaving

\[ \boxed{ \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_\Omega \sigma_{ij}\delta u_{i,j} \,dV } \]

or, in tensor notation,

\[ \boxed{ \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_\Omega \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\nabla\mathbf u \,dV } \]

This result is important.

Starting from forces acting on the boundary and applying equilibrium, we arrive naturally at a volume integral involving stress and the displacement gradient.

But we have not yet obtained the strain tensor.

The displacement gradient contains two different motions

The displacement gradient can be decomposed into symmetric and antisymmetric parts:

\[ \delta u_{i,j} = \delta\varepsilon_{ij} + \delta\omega_{ij}, \]

where

\[ \delta\varepsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \delta u_{i,j} + \delta u_{j,i} \right) \]

and

\[ \delta\omega_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \delta u_{i,j} - \delta u_{j,i} \right). \]

In tensor form,

\[ \delta\nabla\mathbf u = \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + \delta\boldsymbol{\omega}. \]

The symmetric part,

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \nabla\mathbf u + \nabla\mathbf u^T \right), } \]

is the familiar small-strain tensor.

The antisymmetric part,

\[ \boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \nabla\mathbf u - \nabla\mathbf u^T \right), \]

represents infinitesimal rotation.

The question is now very precise:

Which part of the displacement gradient performs internal work against the stress tensor?

Why rotation disappears from the work

Substitute the decomposition into the work expression:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\nabla\mathbf u = \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\omega}. \]

For the classical Cauchy continuum, the stress tensor is symmetric:

\[ \sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}. \]

Meanwhile, the infinitesimal rotation tensor is antisymmetric:

\[ \delta\omega_{ij} = -\delta\omega_{ji}. \]

The double contraction of a symmetric tensor with an antisymmetric tensor vanishes:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\nabla\mathbf u = \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ \delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_\Omega \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \,dV } \]

and, under equilibrium, this is identified with the internal virtual work:

\[ \boxed{ \delta W_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_\Omega \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \,dV. } \]

Now the strain tensor has appeared.

Why does the strain tensor appear?

This derivation gives a physical answer.

We did not begin by defining the strain tensor and then searching for a use for it.

Instead, we began with the work done by physical tractions.

Through Cauchy’s traction law, the divergence theorem, and equilibrium, the boundary work became

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\nabla\mathbf u. \]

The displacement gradient contains both deformation and rotation.

But because the Cauchy stress tensor is symmetric, the rotational part performs no work:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0. \]

What remains is the symmetric part:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \]

So the small-strain tensor

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \frac12 \left( \nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^T \right) } \]

emerges naturally as the deformation measure that is work-conjugate to the Cauchy stress tensor in the small-strain formulation.

This is one reason the strain tensor occupies such a fundamental position in mechanics.

From internal work to strain energy

The incremental internal work density is

\[ dU = \boldsymbol{\sigma}: d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \]

For linear elastic loading,

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathbb C:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \]

where \(\mathbb C\) is the fourth-order elasticity tensor.

The strain-energy density becomes

\[ U = \int \boldsymbol{\sigma}: d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \]

For linear elasticity,

\[ \boxed{ U = \frac12 \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} } \]

or equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ U = \frac12 \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}: \mathbb C: \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. } \]

Stress and strain are therefore not merely two quantities related by a constitutive equation.

They form an energy-conjugate pair.

Their contraction has the meaning of work or energy per unit volume.

Connection to the Z Diagram

This gives a deeper interpretation of the central part of the Z Diagram:

\[ F \longleftrightarrow \sigma \longleftrightarrow \varepsilon \longleftrightarrow u. \]

The previous article concentrated on

\[ F \longleftrightarrow \sigma, \]

showing how equilibrium connects structural forces with the local stress state.

The present derivation concentrates on

\[ \boxed{ \sigma \longleftrightarrow \varepsilon. } \]

The connection is fundamentally energetic:

\[ dU = \boldsymbol{\sigma}: d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. \]

At the material-point scale, stress and strain are energy-conjugate quantities.

At the structural scale, force and displacement form the corresponding work pair.

Schematically,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf u \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{\sigma}: d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} } \]

or, for the whole body,

\[ dW_{\mathrm{external}} = \int_\Omega dW_{\mathrm{internal}}. \]

This is one of the reasons energy provides such a powerful way of connecting the different quantities appearing in structural mechanics.

Beyond small strain

The same idea continues into finite-deformation mechanics, but the appropriate deformation measure depends on the configuration being used.

Current configuration

Let the deformed body occupy \(\Omega_t\).

The external power due to surface traction is

\[ P_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_{\partial\Omega_t} \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf v \,da. \]

Using Cauchy’s traction law and the divergence theorem gives

\[ P_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_{\Omega_t} \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \nabla\mathbf v \,dv. \]

The velocity gradient can be decomposed as

\[ \nabla\mathbf v = \mathbf d+\mathbf w, \]

where

\[ \mathbf d = \frac12 \left( \nabla\mathbf v+\nabla\mathbf v^T \right) \]

is the rate-of-deformation tensor and

\[ \mathbf w = \frac12 \left( \nabla\mathbf v-\nabla\mathbf v^T \right) \]

is the spin tensor.

Again, because

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}^T, \]

we have

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}:\mathbf w=0. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ P_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_{\Omega_t} \boldsymbol{\sigma}:\mathbf d \,dv. } \]

The Cauchy stress \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) and the rate-of-deformation tensor \(\mathbf d\) form the corresponding power-conjugate pair in the current configuration.

Reference configuration

For finite deformation, introduce the deformation gradient

\[ \mathbf F = \frac{\partial\mathbf x}{\partial\mathbf X} \]

and

\[ J=\det\mathbf F. \]

The First Piola–Kirchhoff stress is

\[ \mathbf P = J\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf F^{-T}. \]

The power expression referred to the reference configuration can be written as

\[ \boxed{ P_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_{\Omega_0} \mathbf P:\dot{\mathbf F} \,dV. } \]

Thus,

\[ \mathbf P \quad\text{and}\quad \dot{\mathbf F} \]

form another work-conjugate pair.

Alternatively, define the Green–Lagrange strain

\[ \mathbf E = \frac12 \left( \mathbf F^T\mathbf F-\mathbf I \right). \]

Its work-conjugate stress is the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress \(\mathbf S\), giving

\[ \boxed{ P_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_{\Omega_0} \mathbf S:\dot{\mathbf E} \,dV. } \]

So the particular stress and strain measures change with the formulation, but the underlying requirement remains the same:

the stress and deformation measures must form a consistent work-conjugate pair.

One principle, several formulations

The small- and finite-deformation expressions can now be viewed together.

For small strain,

\[ \boxed{ \int_{\partial\Omega} \mathbf t\cdot\delta\mathbf u\,dA = \int_\Omega \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \,dV. } \]

For finite deformation in the current configuration,

\[ \boxed{ \int_{\partial\Omega_t} \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf v\,da = \int_{\Omega_t} \boldsymbol{\sigma}:\mathbf d\,dv. } \]

And in the reference configuration,

\[ \boxed{ \int_{\Omega_0} \mathbf P:\dot{\mathbf F}\,dV = \int_{\Omega_0} \mathbf S:\dot{\mathbf E}\,dV. } \]

The mathematical quantities change, but the energetic principle connecting external action to internal deformation remains.

Takeaway

Why does the strain tensor appear in mechanics?

Start from the work of surface tractions:

\[ \mathbf t\cdot\delta\mathbf u. \]

Use Cauchy’s traction law, equilibrium, and the divergence theorem, and the corresponding volume quantity becomes

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\nabla\mathbf u. \]

Then decompose the displacement gradient:

\[ \delta\nabla\mathbf u = \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + \delta\boldsymbol{\omega}. \]

Because stress is symmetric,

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0. \]

What remains is

\[ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\sigma}: \delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. } \]

Thus the strain tensor is not merely a convenient symmetrization of the displacement gradient.

It emerges naturally from the mechanics of internal work.

And this leads to one of the central energy relationships in solid mechanics:

\[ \boxed{ dU = \boldsymbol{\sigma}: d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}. } \]
