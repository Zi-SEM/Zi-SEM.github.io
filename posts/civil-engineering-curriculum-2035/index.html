What Should We Teach Civil Engineers?

Rethinking the Civil Engineering Curriculum Beyond Specialization

Engineering Perspectives
Digital Transformation & AI
What should civil engineers learn in the AI era? This article traces the evolution of civil engineering education from practice and engineering science to specialization, and proposes a curriculum architecture that reconnects fundamentals, infrastructure systems, professional depth, AI, energy, climate, and engineering judgment.
Author

Goangseup Zi

Published

September 29, 2026

Keywords

civil engineering curriculum, civil engineering education, future of civil engineering education, civil engineering curriculum reform, AI in civil engineering education, engineering science education, civil engineering systems thinking, infrastructure systems education, civil engineering specialization, civil engineering fundamentals, civil engineering and artificial intelligence, future civil engineer, engineering education reform, systems engineering in civil engineering, civil engineering body of knowledge, ABET civil engineering criteria

English | 한글

I have been thinking about the civil engineering curriculum for a long time.

One question keeps coming back: What exactly are we trying to educate a civil engineer to do?

The question sounds simple, but it becomes difficult as soon as we look at a modern curriculum. Structural engineering, geotechnical engineering, water resources, transportation, environmental engineering, construction, sustainability, resilience, energy, data science, digital twins, and now AI all compete for limited space.

The usual response is to add another course.

That cannot continue indefinitely.

Before asking what new subject should be added, I think we need to ask a more fundamental question:

What is Civil Engineering, and what kind of curriculum follows from that identity?

A useful place to begin is with a distinction that is sometimes blurred in everyday discussion.

Civil Engineering and Construction Engineering are not the same field.

Construction is essential to civil engineering. A bridge, tunnel, dam, or port that cannot actually be built has little engineering value. But construction is one part of a much broader discipline.

ASCE lists construction alongside transportation, coastal, structural, environmental, geotechnical, architectural engineering, engineering mechanics, and other civil engineering specialties. ABET makes the distinction even more explicit: its 2026–2027 criteria provide separate program criteria for Civil and Similarly Named Engineering Programs and Construction and Similarly Named Engineering Programs.

Civil Engineering is therefore not simply the engineering of construction. Its scope is much broader.

That distinction matters when we design a curriculum.

What Does “Civil” in Civil Engineering Mean?

It is worth going back to the name itself.

The Institution of Civil Engineers identifies John Smeaton as the first person known to have described himself as a civil engineer. The term distinguished his work from military engineering. Smeaton’s work was not confined to one type of construction: it included lighthouses, bridges, canals, harbors, drainage works, dams, and water-powered machinery.

From the beginning, Civil Engineering was therefore associated with a broad class of engineered works needed by society.

The modern scope is even wider. Civil engineers deal with buildings, roads and bridges, drinking-water systems, energy systems, ports, airports, environmental infrastructure, and the systems required to keep them functioning.

The object of Civil Engineering is not simply a structure.

It is increasingly an infrastructure system that allows society to function.

That difference becomes important when we ask what students should learn.

When Building It Was the Central Problem

Civil engineering education in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was much closer to professional practice than most curricula are today.

Surveying, engineering drawing, strength of materials, hydraulics, construction materials, bridge design, route location, and field and laboratory work were central parts of the education.

If I reduce the educational question of that period to one sentence—admittedly an oversimplification—it would be:

How do we build it?

This does not mean that early Civil Engineering was equivalent to what we now call Construction Engineering. The professional division among surveying, analysis, design, and construction was less fragmented. A civil engineer often followed a much larger portion of the problem from beginning to end.

Then engineering education changed profoundly.

After World War II: The Rise of Engineering Science

World War II accelerated developments in radar, aircraft, electronics, nuclear technology, advanced materials, and many other technologies. Experience and accumulated know-how were no longer enough to address the new class of engineering problems.

The 1955 ASEE report commonly known as the Grinter Report is one of the landmark documents associated with this transformation of engineering education.

Mathematics, physics, and engineering science took a much stronger position in engineering curricula.

For Civil Engineering, the intellectual structure increasingly looked something like this:

Mathematics
     ↓
Basic Science
     ↓
Engineering Mechanics
     ↓
Solid / Fluid / Materials
     ↓
Analysis
     ↓
Design

The central question shifted.

From

How do we build it?

we increasingly moved toward

Why does it work?

This was a major advance.

Learning how to calculate a particular bridge is very different from learning mechanics well enough to analyze a bridge configuration that one has never seen before.

Engineering Science brought transferable principles behind individual design and construction practices into the center of engineering education.

Civil Engineering advanced enormously as a result.

But that success produced another problem.

Specialization Made Civil Engineering Powerful

Consider structural engineering alone.

Structural Analysis
        ↓
Structural Mechanics
        ↓
RC / Steel
        ↓
FEM
        ↓
Structural Dynamics
        ↓
Earthquake Engineering
        ↓
Reliability
        ↓
Computational Mechanics

The same development occurred in geotechnical engineering, water resources, transportation, environmental engineering, and other areas.

As each field became deeper, it became increasingly unrealistic for one person to master the current technical knowledge of all Civil Engineering specialties.

This is not a failure.

Specialization is one of the consequences of the success of modern Civil Engineering.

The problem comes next.

Universities also organized this specialized knowledge into separate courses and subdisciplines. Students learn structures, geotechnics, hydraulics, transportation, environmental engineering, and construction at different times, often from different faculty members.

But a bridge does not exist that way.

A bridge has a structural system, foundations and soil, hydrology, transportation demand, construction constraints, maintenance requirements, environmental consequences, risk, and economics—all at the same time.

Universities divide knowledge into subjects. Infrastructure does not.

Biot Saw the Problem More Than Sixty Years Ago

Maurice A. Biot addressed this problem forcefully in his 1962 Timoshenko Medal acceptance address, Science and the Engineer, later published in Engineering and Science in 1963.

One sentence is particularly relevant:

“Modern engineering, by its very nature, must be synthetic.”

Biot went further, warning that specialization carried to an extreme becomes a form of “death and decay.”

It would be a mistake to read this as an argument against specialization.

Modern bridge engineering, geotechnical engineering, computational mechanics, and every other advanced engineering field could not have reached their present levels without specialization.

The key word is synthetic.

We must be able to divide a problem deeply enough to develop specialized knowledge—and then put the pieces back together when solving a real engineering problem.

This, to me, captures one of the central problems facing Civil Engineering education today:

Specialization made Civil Engineering powerful. The next educational challenge is learning how to put it back together.

And New Subjects Keep Arriving

The traditional Civil Engineering disciplines have not disappeared. At the same time, new expectations continue to enter the curriculum.

Traditional Civil Engineering
        +
Sustainability
Climate Change
Resilience
Risk
Energy
BIM
Digital Twin
Sensors
Data Science
Artificial Intelligence
Robotics
Cybersecurity

What should we do?

Add an AI course.

Add a digital-twin course.

Add an energy course.

Add a climate course.

If we continue this way, the curriculum eventually becomes impossible to sustain.

The opposite strategy is not much better. If students receive a shallow introduction to everything, we risk producing graduates who have studied many subjects but cannot actually solve a difficult problem in any of them.

The real problem is therefore not simply which course to add or remove.

It is a problem of curriculum architecture.

ABET Does Not Require One Course for Every New Competency

The current ABET criteria are useful here.

The 2026–2027 Civil Engineering Program Criteria include mathematics through differential equations, probability and statistics, calculus-based physics, chemistry, computer or data science, engineering mechanics, materials science, numerical methods, sustainability, risk, resilience, engineering design, and ethics. Students must also solve complex engineering problems in at least four civil engineering specialty areas.

That is already a broad intellectual territory.

But an important point appears in ABET General Criterion 5: the curriculum requirements specify subject areas, not prescribed courses.

The distinction matters.

If AI becomes an essential competency, it does not automatically follow that every program needs another three-credit course called Introduction to AI for Civil Engineers.

The same applies to risk, sustainability, resilience, and other cross-cutting competencies. They can appear repeatedly inside existing courses, design projects, laboratories, and integrated problems.

In many cases, that may be closer to the way engineering actually works.

We Do Not Need to Finish a Civil Engineer in Four Years

There is another assumption worth questioning:

that an undergraduate program must produce a finished Civil Engineer.

ASCE’s Civil Engineering Body of Knowledge, Third Edition identifies 21 foundational, technical, and professional-practice learning outcomes. But the Body of Knowledge does not treat the undergraduate curriculum as the place where every professional capability must be completed.

The development of a professional civil engineer extends beyond the bachelor’s degree:

Undergraduate Education
        +
Postgraduate Education
        +
Mentored Experience
        +
Self-Development
        ↓
Professional Civil Engineer

That makes sense.

It is impossible to teach structures, geotechnics, water resources, transportation, environmental engineering, construction, AI, energy, and every emerging specialty in serious depth within four undergraduate years.

So perhaps the first question should not be

What can we remove?

but

What capabilities must an undergraduate curriculum establish before specialization continues?

I Would Be Very Careful About Reducing the Fundamentals

When new technologies enter a curriculum, one tempting solution is to reduce older subjects.

Reduce mechanics and add AI.

Reduce mathematics and add software.

Reduce hand calculation and add simulation.

At first glance, this sounds reasonable. Computers are already far better at calculation than we are.

But I tend to reach the opposite conclusion.

The more computation AI performs for us, the more important it becomes to have engineers who can decide whether the result makes engineering sense.

Suppose an AI-assisted analysis reports:

Maximum stress = 230 MPa.

The important questions are no longer limited to reproducing the arithmetic that produced 230 MPa.

Is 230 MPa physically plausible?
Is the load path correct?
Are the boundary conditions correct?
Is the constitutive model appropriate?
Is the numerical model adequate?
What uncertainty is involved?
Is the result consistent with the design code?
What engineering decision follows?

To ask those questions, one needs mechanics.

One needs physics and materials.

One also needs probability, numerical methods, and an understanding of modeling assumptions.

Engineering education in the AI era therefore cannot end with

Equation
   ↓
Calculation
   ↓
Answer

Students need to understand the much longer chain:

Reality
   ↓
Idealization
   ↓
Physical Model
   ↓
Mathematical Model
   ↓
Computational / AI Model
   ↓
Result
   ↓
Verification & Validation
   ↓
Engineering Judgment
   ↓
Decision

No matter how well AI performs the calculations in the middle, the path from Reality to Model and the path from Result back to Decision do not disappear.

If anything, they become more important.

AI Should Not Become Another Isolated Course

In September 2026, ASCE released its AI Roadmap for Application in Civil Engineering, or AI RACE. The roadmap asks how civil engineers can lead the effective and responsible adoption of AI rather than merely follow changes introduced elsewhere.

Engineering education should ask the same question.

Teaching students how to use ChatGPT or exposing them to several machine-learning algorithms is not enough.

AI should increasingly become a computational layer across Civil Engineering rather than another isolated island in the curriculum.

Mechanics → computation → surrogate modeling
Structures → sensing → SHM → digital twin
Surveying → spatial data → computer vision
Water → monitoring → forecasting
Transportation → mobility data → prediction and control
Construction → BIM → automation → AI

Energy and climate can be treated in a similar way.

Offshore Wind Is a Good Example

Consider offshore wind, one of my own research areas.

What specialty does an offshore wind support structure belong to?

Structural engineering?

Certainly.

But that is only the beginning.

Ocean Environment
        +
Structural Mechanics
        +
Geotechnical Engineering
        +
Dynamics
        +
Fatigue
        +
Materials
        +
Construction
        +
Environment
        +
Economics
        +
Energy Systems

Remove any one of these perspectives and it becomes difficult to understand the actual system properly.

So bringing energy into a Civil Engineering curriculum does not necessarily mean adding a stand-alone course called Energy Engineering.

The energy transition itself can become a class of engineering problems through which students reconnect multiple areas of Civil Engineering.

That is much closer to Biot’s idea of synthetic engineering.

So How Should We Rebuild the Curriculum?

From this point onward, I am no longer describing an accreditation requirement or a historical fact. What follows is a proposed curriculum architecture derived from the history and constraints discussed above.

The first question should not be which new course to add. We should first decide what must remain common to all civil engineers, where breadth should be created, where genuine depth should be developed, and how emerging topics should connect to existing engineering knowledge.

I find it useful to think of the curriculum in five layers.

          ENERGY     CLIMATE     AI / DATA
             ↓          ↓           ↓
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Layer 5: INTEGRATING THEMES             │
 │ Energy · Climate · AI · Sustainability │
 │ Risk · Resilience · Digital Engineering│
 └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     ↓
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Layer 4: PROFESSIONAL DEPTH             │
 │ One or more selected specialty tracks  │
 └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     ↓
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Layer 3: CIVIL INFRASTRUCTURE SYSTEMS   │
 │ Structure · Geo · Water · Transport    │
 │ Environment · Construction · Energy    │
 └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     ↓
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Layer 2: ENGINEERING SCIENCE            │
 │ Solid · Fluid · Materials · Numerical  │
 │ Experiment · Computation               │
 └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     ↓
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Layer 1: FOUNDATIONS                    │
 │ Math · Physics · Chemistry · Statistics│
 │ Computing                              │
 └─────────────────────────────────────────┘

This is not a hierarchy of importance. The lower layers make the upper layers possible. More importantly, Layer 5 should not become another bundle of isolated courses. It is a horizontal axis that cuts across all the layers below it.

Layer 1 — Mathematical and Scientific Foundation

This is the part of the curriculum that should remain deepest and most stable.

Area Possible courses
Mathematics Calculus I–II, Linear Algebra, Differential Equations
Probability Probability and Statistics
Basic science Physics, Chemistry
Computing foundation Programming / Scientific Computing

Programming here does not mean learning a particular AI tool. It is better understood as a basic language for numerical methods, data analysis, simulation, and modern engineering computation.

The rise of AI does not make the fundamentals less important. If machines perform more of the calculation and information retrieval, the engineer becomes even more responsible for deciding whether the result is physically plausible.

Layer 2 — Engineering Science

Engineering science provides a common language across civil engineering specialties.

Area Possible courses
Solid mechanics Statics, Dynamics, Mechanics of Materials
Fluid mechanics Fluid Mechanics
Materials Engineering Materials / Material Behavior
Computation Numerical Methods, Computational Methods
Experiment Experimental Methods / Measurement

One of the great achievements of the postwar engineering-science curriculum was to move transferable principles to the center of engineering education rather than teaching only procedures for particular structures or established designs.

That layer is not obsolete. What should change is that numerical and computational thinking should now be recognized more explicitly as part of modern engineering science.

Layer 3 — Civil Infrastructure Systems

Civil engineering breadth has often been created by requiring introductory exposure to Structures, Geotechnical Engineering, Water Resources, Transportation, Environmental Engineering, and Construction.

The difficulty is that as the field expands, adding another small introductory course for every new area simply reproduces fragmentation inside the curriculum.

A different approach is to retain the disciplines but teach students more explicitly how they interact inside functioning infrastructure systems.

Infrastructure system Examples of domains involved
Structural Systems structures, loads, structural behavior
Ground and Underground Systems soil, foundations, underground infrastructure
Water and Environmental Systems hydraulics, hydrology, water, environment
Transportation and Urban Systems transportation, mobility, urban infrastructure
Construction and Infrastructure Management construction, cost, scheduling, safety, maintenance
Coastal and Energy Infrastructure coastal systems, offshore structures, energy infrastructure

A city, for example, does not arrive divided into academic departments.

                     CITY / REGION
                          │
         ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
         │                │                │
       Water        Transportation       Energy
         │                │                │
        Dam ── River ── Bridge ── Tunnel ── Grid
                         │
                    Buildings
                         │
                  Underground
                         │
                 Waste / Environment

The purpose at this layer is not to teach every specialty in depth. It is to make students ask:

How do these subsystems connect to form a functioning infrastructure system?

Layer 4 — Selected Professional Depth

Systems thinking alone is not enough. If integration is emphasized without depth, we simply create a different form of superficial education.

Every student should go deep enough in at least one area to calculate, model, analyze, and design real engineering problems.

Depth track Examples of advanced courses
Structures and Mechanics Structural Analysis, Reinforced Concrete, Steel Structures, FEM, Structural Dynamics
Geotechnical and Underground Soil Mechanics, Foundation Engineering, Underground Engineering
Water and Environment Hydrology, Hydraulic Engineering, Environmental Engineering
Transportation and Urban Systems Transportation Engineering, Traffic Systems, Urban Infrastructure
Construction and Infrastructure Management Construction Engineering, Project Management, Infrastructure Management
Coastal and Energy Infrastructure Coastal Engineering, Offshore Engineering, Energy Infrastructure

These are implementation examples, not accreditation requirements. Universities can organize the tracks differently according to their mission, faculty, region, and students.

The principle matters more than the exact list.

Breadth allows an engineer to see the whole problem. Depth allows an engineer to actually solve part of it.

We cannot teach everything deeply. But we should not graduate students who have never gone deeply into anything.

Layer 5 — Integrating Themes

This is perhaps the easiest layer to misunderstand.

If AI, energy, and climate matter, the immediate reaction is often to create a new vertical silo for each of them. I do not think that is the right architecture.

Theme Examples of curriculum integration
AI and Data sensing, prediction, surrogate modeling, SHM, digital twins
Energy offshore wind, hydropower, nuclear infrastructure, energy storage
Climate flooding, coastal hazards, adaptation
Sustainability materials, life cycle, embodied carbon
Risk and Resilience uncertainty, reliability, hazards, recovery
Digital Engineering BIM, simulation, digital twins, automation

AI can appear in mechanics through computation and surrogate modeling, in structures through sensing and SHM, in surveying through spatial data and computer vision, and in construction through BIM and automation.

Energy similarly cuts across structural, geotechnical, hydraulic, coastal, and environmental engineering.

Layer 5 should therefore be understood as a horizontal mechanism for reconnecting established civil engineering knowledge to new societal and technological problems, not as another collection of stand-alone courses.

A Project Spine Can Connect the Layers Vertically

Where, then, do the five layers meet in a student’s education?

One possibility is to use a small number of complex infrastructure projects repeatedly across the four undergraduate years. I call this a Project Spine.

For example:

Project A — City Infrastructure

Water
Transportation
Structures
Underground
Environment
Energy

Project B — Bridge / Transportation Corridor

Structures
Geotechnics
Transportation
Hydrology
Construction
Maintenance
Risk

Project C — Offshore Renewable Energy System

Ocean Environment
Structural Mechanics
Geotechnics
Dynamics
Fatigue
Construction
Energy
Environment
Economics
AI / Digital Twin

The important point is not to complete a project once and move on. The same infrastructure system should be revisited at increasing levels of sophistication.

Year 1 → What is the system?
Year 2 → What physics governs it?
Year 3 → How do we analyze and design it?
Year 4 → How do we integrate, optimize, and make decisions?

In the first year, students learn to see the system. In the second, they encounter the governing physics. In the third, they move into analysis and design. In the fourth, they put the subsystems back together and make decisions under uncertainty and competing requirements.

The Project Spine therefore cuts vertically through the five-layer architecture.

        INTEGRATING THEMES
               │
        PROFESSIONAL DEPTH
               │
    CIVIL INFRASTRUCTURE SYSTEMS
               │
        ENGINEERING SCIENCE
               │
            FOUNDATION
               │
          PROJECT SPINE
               ↓
       ENGINEERING JUDGMENT

That seems much more like civil engineering than responding to every new technology by adding another course.

The goal of this architecture is not to produce a student who knows a little about everything.

It is to develop an engineer with strong fundamentals, an understanding of infrastructure as a system, genuine depth in at least one professional domain, and the ability to connect AI, energy, climate, and other emerging changes back to established engineering knowledge.

That is how breadth and depth can coexist without turning the curriculum into an ever-growing list of courses.

Civil Engineering Is Broader Than Construction

The terminology used for the field varies across countries and universities, and construction remains one of its essential professional activities. But the curriculum itself makes the broader identity clear.

Civil engineers must increasingly deal not only with creating new infrastructure but also with maintaining aging infrastructure, adapting systems to climate change, supporting energy transition, monitoring performance, managing risk, and integrating physical infrastructure with data and AI.

Construction remains essential, but it is too narrow to describe the full purpose of Civil Engineering.

That broader identity should be visible in the curriculum.

In the End, We Come Back to Civil Engineering

Civil Engineering has spent more than a century differentiating itself into increasingly sophisticated specialties.

That differentiation gave the profession depth.

There is no reason to give that depth up.

But students also need to learn how to reconnect the pieces.

Natural Environment
        ↕
Physical Infrastructure
        ↕
Human Society
        ↕
Economy / Policy
        ↕
Information / Data

Calling Civil Engineering a broad or synthetic discipline should not mean that students simply take many courses.

It should mean that they can connect different bodies of knowledge to understand a real infrastructure problem and make a defensible engineering decision.

That, I think, is the point behind Biot’s warning more than sixty years ago.

The answer is not less specialization.

The answer is not losing the ability to synthesize specialized knowledge.

A More Important Question Than “What Else Should We Teach?”

New technologies will continue to arrive.

Today it is AI. Tomorrow it will be something else.

A curriculum cannot remain coherent if every new development automatically becomes another course.

I would therefore begin curriculum design with three questions.

What must remain even when technology changes?

Fundamentals and Engineering Science.

Where must students develop real depth?

In a selected professional domain.

What must be reconnected?

Infrastructure systems with AI, data, energy, climate, sustainability, and risk.

The curriculum can then be summarized as

FOUNDATION
     +
ENGINEERING SCIENCE
     +
SYSTEMS BREADTH
     +
SELECTED DEPTH
     +
AI / ENERGY / CLIMATE INTEGRATION
     +
ENGINEERING JUDGMENT

And at the end of that sequence, one capability remains fundamental:

Does this result make engineering sense?

AI may perform more and more of the calculation, but that question will not disappear.

It will become more important.

That is where I believe the future of Civil Engineering education lies.

Specialization made Civil Engineering powerful. The next educational challenge is learning how to put it back together.
