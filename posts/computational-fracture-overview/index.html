Why Is Computational Fracture So Difficult?

From remeshing to XFEM and XEFG: representing a discontinuity that moves through the numerical approximation

A crack creates a peculiar problem for computational mechanics.

Before cracking, we normally approximate a continuous displacement field over a body. After a crack appears, the displacement field can become discontinuous across an internal surface. The crack may then grow, turn, branch, join another crack, or cut through elements that were never designed to contain it.

The physical body changes its internal topology while the numerical mesh would prefer to remain unchanged.

That is the central difficulty.

I was very active in computational fracture for many years. Much of my work in this field involved the extended finite element method (XFEM) and its meshfree counterpart, the extended element-free Galerkin method (XEFG), together with dynamic fracture, cohesive cracks, multiple-crack growth, three-dimensional crack tracking, phantom-node formulations, and related methods.

Looking back, I think the most useful way to explain this work is not paper by paper.

The engineering question is:

How can a numerical approximation represent a crack that moves independently of the discretization?

That question connects a large part of the work.

Why ordinary finite elements have difficulty with cracks

In a standard finite element approximation,

\[ \mathbf{u}^h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_I N_I(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}_I, \]

the interpolation is built from shape functions attached to the mesh.

This is extremely effective as long as the displacement field has the regularity assumed by the interpolation.

A crack changes that.

Across a crack surface, the displacement may jump:

\[ [\![\mathbf{u}]\!] \neq \mathbf{0}. \]

Near a classical crack tip, the stress and strain fields can also contain strong gradients or singular asymptotic behavior.

If the crack is forced to follow element boundaries, the numerical representation becomes easy, but the mesh begins to influence the crack path.

If the crack is allowed to cut arbitrarily through elements, then the approximation must somehow represent a discontinuity that is not built into the original mesh connectivity.

Historically, one solution was remeshing:

grow crack
→ modify mesh
→ project state variables
→ solve again
→ grow crack
→ remesh again

This can work, but repeated remeshing is burdensome. It introduces projection between successive meshes, complicates history-dependent problems, and makes multiple cracks, branching, and dynamic propagation increasingly difficult.

The deeper idea behind XFEM was to avoid making the mesh carry all the geometric information about the crack.

The key idea: separate the crack from the mesh

The ordinary finite element space can be enriched with additional functions that contain information about the discontinuity.

A schematic enriched approximation is

\[ \mathbf{u}^h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_I N_I(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}_I + \sum_{J\in\mathcal{N}_c} N_J(\mathbf{x})\Psi_J(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{a}_J, \]

where the first term is the ordinary approximation and the second provides additional degrees of freedom associated with the crack.

The important feature is conceptual:

background mesh
    represents the body

enrichment
    represents information missing from the ordinary approximation

The crack is therefore allowed to pass through an element without requiring that the element boundary coincide with the crack.

Figure Figure 1 looks simple, but it contains the essential shift in viewpoint.

The crack is an internal geometric object.

The mesh is a discretization device.

They do not have to be the same object.

Do not force the mesh to represent the crack. Give the approximation enough information to represent the crack independently of the mesh.

This became one of the most powerful ideas in computational fracture.

Why the crack tip is more difficult than the crack interior

Representing a fully developed displacement jump is relatively straightforward.

The crack tip is harder.

Along the open part of the crack, the two sides are separated. At the tip, however, the crack opening must close properly.

This means that simply enriching every cut element with the same discontinuous function does not automatically produce the correct kinematics near the tip.

One of my early contributions with Ted Belytschko addressed precisely this issue for cohesive cracks.

Instead of treating the crack-tip element as an awkward exception that required the conventional branch-function construction, we developed new crack-tip formulations that allowed the crack to end inside an element while maintaining the required crack closure.

That work had a practical objective:

make arbitrary crack growth easier to implement while retaining a clean enriched representation.

This concern reappeared repeatedly in later methods, including XEFG and phantom-node formulations.

The crack interior is a discontinuity problem.

The crack tip is simultaneously a discontinuity-termination problem and a propagation problem.

Crack geometry also has to move

Once the approximation can represent a crack, another question appears:

Where is the crack?

For a growing crack, the geometry itself must evolve.

Level-set descriptions became useful because the crack could be represented by nodal scalar fields rather than by constantly rebuilding mesh topology.

For example, one field can describe the signed distance normal to the crack and another can help locate the tip.

This creates an important separation:

finite element mesh
    ↓
approximation space

level-set or geometric representation
    ↓
crack position and crack tip

fracture criterion
    ↓
where the crack moves next

The numerical method therefore needs more than a stress calculation.

It needs a representation of evolving geometry.

Dynamic fracture makes the problem even harder

In static crack growth, a crack may be advanced incrementally after checking a fracture criterion.

In dynamic fracture, the crack propagates while inertia is important.

Now several questions are coupled:

  • When does the crack start?
  • In which direction does it move?
  • How fast does it move?
  • How is cohesive energy dissipated?
  • How should the discontinuity be updated during time integration?
  • Can the crack path remain independent of the element orientation?

Our XFEM work on dynamic fracture used local partition-of-unity enrichment together with level sets so that arbitrary crack paths could be represented without remeshing.

Figure Figure 2 shows the numerical idea directly.

The crack moves.

The background mesh does not.

That may sound obvious today, but it addresses one of the most persistent practical problems in numerical fracture.

Multiple cracks change the topology, not just the geometry

A single smooth crack is only the beginning.

Real fracture can involve:

  • several cracks initiating at different locations,
  • crack competition,
  • crack shielding,
  • coalescence,
  • branching,
  • crack junctions,
  • and eventually a connected fracture network.

In our work on multiple crack growth, XFEM was used together with implicit crack descriptions so that cracks could grow and interconnect without remeshing.

This is a much more demanding problem than advancing one crack tip.

When two cracks join, the topology of the discontinuity network changes.

A good numerical representation should not require a completely different algorithm every time that topology changes.

This is one reason enrichment-based approaches became attractive: the mesh can remain a background structure while the discontinuity network evolves separately.

From XFEM to XEFG

The next question was natural.

If enrichment can be added to finite element approximation spaces, can the same concept be used with meshfree approximations?

This led to work on the extended element-free Galerkin method (XEFG).

Meshfree methods already avoid fixed element connectivity and use domains of influence around particles. That gives them attractive properties for large deformation and evolving discontinuities, but traditional meshfree crack treatments also had difficulties, particularly near crack tips and in maintaining accurate discontinuous fields.

XEFG imports the partition-of-unity enrichment idea into the meshfree approximation.

The comparison is conceptually useful.

In the meshfree setting, the support of a particle may be fully cut, partially cut, or contain the crack tip.

That makes the kinematic problem somewhat different from the finite element case.

Our XEFG work addressed cohesive cracks in static and dynamic settings, later extending the concept to three-dimensional crack initiation, propagation, branching, and junction.

The physical problem remained the same.

The numerical representation changed.

Why branch enrichment became an important issue

Classical XFEM for linear elastic fracture mechanics often uses analytical near-tip branch functions motivated by the asymptotic crack-tip solution.

This can provide excellent accuracy when the asymptotic field is appropriate.

But cohesive fracture raises a different question.

If the crack-tip behavior is governed by a cohesive law rather than the classical traction-free LEFM singular field, should we always impose the classical branch enrichment?

Our work explored formulations in which cohesive cracks could be represented without conventional crack-tip branch enrichment.

That was not merely an attempt to shorten an equation.

It was an attempt to make the numerical representation more closely reflect the assumed fracture mechanics.

This distinction is important:

An enrichment function is not just a numerical trick. It is prior information inserted into the approximation space.

Therefore the enrichment should be compatible with the physics we are trying to represent.

Three-dimensional fracture changes the difficulty again

A crack in two dimensions is represented by a line.

A crack in three dimensions is a surface bounded by a crack front.

That seemingly small change creates several major numerical problems.

The method must describe:

  • a moving surface,
  • a moving crack front,
  • local crack-front orientation,
  • propagation direction along that front,
  • curved surfaces,
  • branching surfaces,
  • and junctions between cracks.

Our later XEFG and partition-of-unity work placed significant emphasis on this three-dimensional problem.

The difficulty is not just computational cost.

It is geometric consistency.

A badly tracked crack surface can produce artificial kinks, discontinuous normals, or incorrect junction topology even if the underlying stress solution is otherwise accurate.

This is why three-dimensional computational fracture is partly a mechanics problem and partly a computational-geometry problem.

Other representations emerged for good reasons

XFEM and XEFG were not the end of computational fracture.

Some later work in the uploaded collection explores related or alternative representations:

  • phantom-node methods,
  • cracking-particle approaches,
  • extended isogeometric formulations,
  • multiscale coupling,
  • peridynamics,
  • and phase-field fracture.

These approaches make different choices about where the discontinuity is represented.

A sharp enriched method says, in effect:

the crack is geometrically sharp
and the approximation is modified around it

A phase-field model says:

replace the sharp crack by a narrow continuous damage zone

Peridynamics reformulates the mechanics through nonlocal interactions so that bond failure can represent fracture without requiring the classical local PDE structure at the crack.

A phantom-node formulation represents separated material portions through overlapping element descriptions rather than explicit enrichment functions.

These are not merely competing numerical brands.

They are different answers to the same fundamental question:

How should a changing discontinuity be represented inside a numerical model?

That question is more fundamental than the name of the method.

My computational-fracture work forms a sequence

Looking across the papers, I see a fairly clear progression.

1. Make arbitrary cracks independent of the mesh

Early XFEM work addressed cohesive crack tips, dynamic cracks, and multiple crack growth without remeshing.

2. Make the representation robust when cracks grow and interact

The work expanded to crack junctions, coalescence, dynamic propagation, and alternative tip treatments.

3. Move the enrichment idea into meshfree approximation

XEFG introduced discontinuous enrichment into element-free Galerkin approximations and developed it for static, dynamic, and three-dimensional cohesive fracture.

4. Extend the geometry and application domain

Later studies treated three-dimensional crack surfaces, reinforced concrete, shells, piezoelectric materials, and multiscale problems.

5. Explore alternative fracture representations

Phantom-node methods, cracking particles, peridynamics, and phase-field formulations reflect the broader evolution of computational fracture.

For this reason, I do not think my computational-fracture work should be compressed into a single article.

The broader mechanics lesson

The most interesting lesson from XFEM is not merely that cracks can be simulated without remeshing.

It is a more general computational-mechanics principle:

When the ordinary approximation cannot represent an important feature of the solution, enrich the approximation with the missing information rather than forcing the discretization to imitate the physics.

For fracture, the missing information is the discontinuity and, depending on the formulation, the crack-tip behavior.

For other problems, the missing information may be an interface, a singularity, a localized mode, or another known feature of the solution.

That is why the ideas developed in computational fracture have importance beyond fracture itself.

The mesh is only a numerical scaffold.

The approximation should represent the mechanics.
