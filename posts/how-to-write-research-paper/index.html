How Should an Engineer Write a Research Paper?

Creativity, consistency, and completeness—from the research question to publication

LaTeX & Writing
Scientific Writing
Research Writing
A practical engineering approach to journal writing built around the CCC rule: Creativity, Consistency, and Completeness.
Author

Goangseup Zi

Published

September 3, 2026

A research paper is not simply a record of what we did.

It is a carefully constructed route that allows another researcher to understand what question was asked, why it matters, what was done, what was found, and why the answer should be believed.

I often describe writing this way:

Writing is like paving a highway from your brain to the reader.

The reader cannot see the calculations on your desk, the failed experiments, the intermediate arguments, or the discussions that shaped the work. The paper must provide enough of the road for the reader to travel from the research question to the conclusion without getting lost.

Over the years, I have found it useful to reduce good research writing to three requirements:

The CCC rule

Creativity
Consistency
Completeness

These three words provide a useful test not only for the finished manuscript but also for the research itself.

  • Creativity: Is there something genuinely new?
  • Consistency: Does every part of the paper tell the same story?
  • Completeness: Is every essential logical step present?

A paper can be written in grammatically excellent English and still fail if one of these is missing.

1. Why publish?

Research has limited value if the knowledge remains only in the researcher’s notebook or computer.

Publication converts an individual result into part of the scientific record.

A journal article can:

  • communicate new knowledge to the research community,
  • create a permanent record of an advance,
  • allow others to examine, reproduce, challenge, or extend the work,
  • demonstrate the contribution of the researchers,
  • and connect the work to future research and practice.

For engineers, this is particularly important. Engineering knowledge is cumulative. A model, experiment, numerical method, or design insight becomes useful to the community only when others can understand what was done and under what conditions the result is valid.

A conference presentation may introduce the work, but a journal paper normally carries a different responsibility. It should provide enough evidence and explanation for the result to become part of the technical literature.

2. Before writing: is there really a paper?

Before worrying about grammar, figures, or journal format, ask a more fundamental question:

Are the results worth publishing?

In my lectures, I reduce this to one keyword:

NEW

A publishable contribution may contain:

  • a new theory,
  • a new experimental observation,
  • a new numerical method,
  • a new application of an established principle,
  • a new interpretation of known results,
  • a new mechanism,
  • or a useful engineering consequence that was not previously understood.

Novelty does not always mean inventing an entirely new field.

A new interpretation can be important if it changes how engineers understand or use an existing result.

But novelty alone is not sufficient. Before writing, I also ask whether:

  • the procedure is described well enough to reproduce the work,
  • the results are internally consistent,
  • and the reasoning from evidence to conclusion is complete.

These three checks already anticipate the CCC rule.

Creativity: give the reader somewhere worth going

3. Creativity is the reason the paper exists

The first C is Creativity.

A paper should answer:

What is new here?

If the answer is difficult to state, the manuscript is likely to become difficult to write.

The new contribution should be visible throughout the article:

Title
  ↓
Abstract
  ↓
Introduction
  ↓
Research question
  ↓
Method
  ↓
Results
  ↓
Discussion
  ↓
Conclusions

The form changes from section to section, but the contribution should remain recognizable.

For example, the introduction may identify the unresolved problem. The method explains what was done to address it. The results provide the evidence. The discussion explains the meaning. The conclusions state what has been learned.

This is why I prefer to think of a research article as a story of a question and its answer, rather than as a chronological diary of everything that happened during the project.

4. Choose the journal for the work—not the other way around

Journal choice matters, but it should not dominate the research story.

Important considerations include:

  • whether the journal covers the subject,
  • the scientific and engineering audience,
  • reputation and quality,
  • review and publication speed,
  • paper-length limitations,
  • and whether the expected readership is likely to use or cite the work.

The journal’s aims and scope should always be checked.

A technically strong paper can still be rejected quickly if it does not belong to the journal.

However, it is not always necessary to choose the final journal before writing the first complete draft.

I prefer first to make the research story clear. Publisher formatting can come later.

The scientific argument should not be distorted merely to fit a template too early.

Consistency: keep the entire paper on the same road

5. A journal article tells one story

The second C is Consistency.

The title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and conclusions must describe the same research.

This may sound obvious, but inconsistency is one of the most common weaknesses in technical manuscripts.

For example:

  • the title promises one topic,
  • the introduction motivates another,
  • the results emphasize a third,
  • and the conclusions suddenly make a claim that was never demonstrated.

A strong paper has a visible line of continuity:

Problem
→ Question
→ Method
→ Evidence
→ Interpretation
→ Answer

Consistency also applies at a more detailed level.

Check that the manuscript uses consistent:

  • terminology,
  • mathematical symbols,
  • sign conventions,
  • units,
  • abbreviations,
  • numerical values,
  • figure labels,
  • table values,
  • terminology for specimens or models,
  • and writing style.

In engineering papers, a small inconsistency can become a technical error.

If a load is defined as positive upward in one section and implicitly treated as positive downward in another, the problem is not stylistic. It affects interpretation.

6. Build an outline before writing paragraphs

Before drafting a long manuscript, I recommend making an outline.

The exact outline depends on the work.

A numerical study might be organized as:

Problem
→ governing formulation
→ numerical method
→ verification
→ parameter study
→ engineering interpretation

An experimental paper may instead follow:

Problem
→ materials/specimens
→ test setup
→ loading and measurement
→ observations
→ interpretation

A materials paper may require another structure.

The purpose of the outline is not to impose a rigid format. It is to see the logical sequence before spending time polishing sentences.

A useful test is:

If I read only the section headings, do I understand how the paper intends to answer its question?

If not, the structure probably needs more work.

7. The title: define the subject before attracting attention

The title is often the first part of the paper that a potential reader sees.

It should be:

  • clear,
  • informative,
  • specific enough to define the subject,
  • but not overloaded with unnecessary detail.

A title should usually be a compact phrase rather than a full sentence.

The challenge is balance.

Too general:

Analysis of Concrete Structures

Too detailed titles may become difficult to read and hide the main contribution.

A better title usually identifies the important mechanism, method, problem, or application without trying to reproduce the abstract.

A good question to ask is:

After reading only the title, will the right reader know that this paper may be relevant?

8. The abstract: the entire paper in miniature

The abstract is not an introduction.

It is a condensed representation of the whole paper.

Many readers who see the title will read only the abstract before deciding whether to continue. Therefore the abstract should normally contain the essential elements of the study:

Context or problem
→ motivation
→ what was done
→ how it was done
→ important result
→ significance

The abstract should not contain every result.

It should contain all necessary information, but not unnecessary detail.

For most journal papers, the abstract is one compact paragraph and is often roughly 100–250 words, although the journal’s instructions govern the actual limit.

Citations are generally avoided unless absolutely necessary.

A useful test is:

Can a reader understand the question, approach, principal finding, and significance without reading the paper?

If not, the abstract is incomplete.

If the abstract contains many secondary details but the main result is difficult to find, it is overloaded.

9. The introduction: narrow the road toward the question

The introduction should bring the reader from the general technical context to the specific research question.

A useful progression is:

Topic
→ engineering/scientific problem
→ relevant previous work
→ what is already known
→ what remains unresolved
→ what this paper does
→ why it matters

I often recommend beginning with a strong topic sentence that tells the reader immediately what area of the problem is important.

The literature review should not be a list of papers.

Its purpose is to establish the state of knowledge needed to understand the gap.

The introduction should make three things particularly clear:

  1. What is the problem?
  2. What has not yet been resolved?
  3. What does this paper do about it?

Near the end, it is often helpful to state the scope and structure of the paper.

The reader should arrive at the method already knowing why that method is needed.

Completeness: make sure the highway has no missing sections

10. Completeness does not mean including everything

The third C is Completeness.

This is often misunderstood.

Completeness does not mean writing the longest possible paper.

Completeness ≠ maximum information

It means:

No essential logical step is missing between the research question and the conclusion.

Therefore:

Completeness
=
everything necessary for the reader
to reach and evaluate the conclusion

Routine calculations may be omitted.

Standard procedures may be cited rather than reproduced in full.

Secondary observations may be left out if they do not contribute to the paper’s purpose.

But an assumption, boundary condition, measurement procedure, validation step, or piece of evidence that is necessary to support the conclusion cannot simply disappear.

This distinction is especially important in engineering, because results depend on conditions.

A number without the assumptions and conditions that produced it may be technically meaningless.

11. Procedure or methods: could someone reproduce the work?

The method section should explain how the results were obtained.

For theoretical or computational work, this may include:

  • hypotheses,
  • governing equations,
  • assumptions,
  • constitutive models,
  • boundary and initial conditions,
  • numerical implementation,
  • solution procedures,
  • and verification examples.

For experimental work, it may include:

  • materials,
  • specimen geometry,
  • test equipment,
  • loading conditions,
  • instrumentation,
  • measurement procedures,
  • calibration,
  • and data processing.

The method should usually follow a logical sequence.

A useful standard is:

Could a competent researcher in the field reproduce the essential result from the information provided?

This does not require documenting every arithmetic operation or every routine laboratory action.

The objective is reproducibility without obscuring the main flow.

The reader needs the information that controls the result.

12. Results: show what the study actually produced

The results section answers:

What did we find?

Figures and tables are especially important here because engineering results are often easier to understand visually than verbally.

Good results presentation should reveal:

  • trends,
  • differences,
  • correlations,
  • failure modes,
  • parameter effects,
  • validation,
  • uncertainty where relevant,
  • and other observations that support the paper’s answer.

Do not force the reader to extract the principal result from a large mass of numbers.

The hierarchy should be clear.

Primary findings deserve primary visual and textual emphasis.

Secondary findings should not dominate the manuscript.

13. Discussion: explain why the result makes engineering sense

For me, this is one of the most important parts of an engineering paper.

Results tell us what happened.

Discussion tells us what the result means.

A useful discussion contains three elements:

Interpretation
→ answer to the research question
→ engineering implication

The discussion may address:

  • the overall trend,
  • physical or mechanical interpretation,
  • agreement or disagreement with previous work,
  • originality,
  • limitations,
  • engineering significance,
  • recommended use,
  • and implications for future work.

This is also where engineering intuition becomes important.

A numerical curve should not merely be described as increasing or decreasing. Ask why.

A structural response should be connected to stiffness, equilibrium, compatibility, inertia, energy, damage, geometry, or another relevant physical mechanism.

An experimental observation should be connected to a plausible mechanism while clearly distinguishing observation from interpretation.

For this site, I would summarize the principle as:

Do not stop at “what happened?” Ask “why does this result make engineering sense?”

That question often transforms a technically correct paper into a useful engineering paper.

14. Results and discussion do different jobs

Some journals combine Results and Discussion; others separate them.

Either organization can work.

What matters is that the two intellectual functions remain distinguishable.

Result:

The measured stiffness decreased after repeated loading.

Interpretation:

The reduction is consistent with progressive cracking and the resulting loss of effective load-transfer area.

The first statement reports evidence.

The second explains the evidence.

The distinction is important because interpretation may later be revised even when the experimental observation remains valid.

A strong paper makes this boundary visible.

15. Conclusions: answer the question, do not rewrite the paper

The conclusion should summarize the important results concisely and emphasize the contribution.

It should connect directly back to the question established in the introduction.

A useful conclusion can state:

  • what was developed or discovered,
  • the principal findings,
  • what is new,
  • the engineering significance,
  • important limitations,
  • and, where appropriate, practical recommendations.

Do not introduce major new evidence in the conclusion.

Tables and figures are usually unnecessary there because the evidence should already have been presented.

A strong test is:

Can every important statement in the conclusions be traced back to evidence and discussion in the paper?

If not, the conclusion is making a claim that the paper has not earned.

16. Figures and tables should carry the story

A good figure can communicate information that would otherwise require pages of prose.

But figures should not be decorative.

Every figure and table should contribute necessary, nonredundant information to the paper.

I often suggest a severe test:

If someone looked only at the figures, tables, and captions, could they approximately reconstruct the story of the paper?

If the answer is yes, the visual structure is probably strong.

Check that:

  • every figure and table is referred to in the text,
  • labels and legends are legible,
  • units are shown,
  • symbols match the manuscript,
  • captions explain what is being shown,
  • visual information is not unnecessarily repeated,
  • and the key result is easy to identify.

For engineering papers, the strongest sequence is often:

Observation
→ Mechanism
→ Engineering consequence

A figure is valuable when it helps the reader make that transition.

17. References are part of the technical argument

References perform more than a formatting function.

They show:

  • what is already known,
  • where methods originated,
  • how the present work differs from previous studies,
  • and where readers can verify supporting information.

A reference list should include only works that are actually cited.

Every citation should correspond to the correct bibliographic record.

Before submission, check:

  • author names,
  • title,
  • journal or book,
  • year,
  • volume and issue where required,
  • pages,
  • DOI where appropriate,
  • and consistency with the journal’s citation style.

Whether the journal uses author–year or numbered citations, the underlying purpose is the same: a reader should be able to identify the source unambiguously.

18. Plagiarism: give credit precisely

Scientific writing depends on traceability of ideas.

Plagiarism includes not only copying sentences without attribution but also presenting another person’s result or idea as one’s own.

The basic rule is simple:

Give credit where the credit belongs.

If an idea, method, figure, dataset, or result comes from another source, acknowledge it appropriately.

This is true even within a collaborative research group.

Clear attribution does not weaken one’s contribution.

It makes the scientific record more reliable.

It also helps distinguish the genuinely new contribution of the paper from the knowledge on which it builds.

19. Authorship represents contribution and responsibility

Authorship should reflect substantial contribution to the work.

The first author commonly carries the largest direct contribution to the research and manuscript, although practices vary among fields and projects.

The corresponding author has an additional publication responsibility: communication with the journal, submission, revision, responses to reviewers, and coordination of the publication process.

More generally, authorship should be discussed explicitly rather than treated as an automatic reward for seniority or association.

Every author should be able to take responsibility for the work to the extent appropriate to the contribution.

Because current journals increasingly request formal contribution statements, it is useful to clarify roles before submission rather than after disagreements arise.

20. Acknowledgements: record support that is not authorship

Not every important contribution requires authorship.

Acknowledgements may recognize:

  • research funding,
  • technical assistance,
  • laboratory support,
  • useful discussions,
  • data or facility access,
  • and other contributions that should be recorded but do not satisfy authorship requirements.

Funding information should follow the requirements of the funding agency and journal.

From manuscript to publication

21. Style files and journal templates come after the research story

Once the scientific manuscript is mature, check the journal’s guide for authors carefully.

Requirements may include:

  • style or class files,
  • page or word limits,
  • reference format,
  • figure specifications,
  • data availability statements,
  • graphical abstracts,
  • highlights,
  • supplementary files,
  • or specific declarations.

Formatting is necessary.

But formatting is not the scientific contribution.

I prefer to separate the two tasks mentally:

write the paper
        ↓
make the argument complete
        ↓
adapt to the journal

This reduces the risk that attention to formatting hides weaknesses in the research story.

22. Final check: inspect the paper as a system

Before submission, do not proofread only sentence by sentence.

Check the article at several scales.

Story

Does the same question run through:

Title
→ Abstract
→ Introduction
→ Method
→ Results
→ Discussion
→ Conclusions

Technical consistency

Check:

  • symbols,
  • units,
  • numerical values,
  • sample names,
  • parameter values,
  • figure data,
  • table data,
  • equations,
  • and terminology.

Abstract and conclusions

Read these two sections together.

Do they describe the same contribution?

Does every major conclusion appear, in appropriate condensed form, in the abstract?

Figures and tables

Are all cited?

Are they legible?

Do the captions make sense without excessive searching through the text?

References

Is every reference cited?

Is every citation in the reference list?

Is the metadata correct?

Language

Check grammar and spelling, but remember:

Correct English cannot rescue incomplete logic.

Scientific clarity comes first.

23. The cover letter: explain why the editor should care

A cover letter should be short and purposeful.

It normally identifies:

  • the full title of the manuscript,
  • the principal contribution,
  • why the work matters,
  • and why the journal is an appropriate place for it.

Do not simply repeat the abstract.

The editor’s first decision is often whether the paper is appropriate for peer review at all.

A useful cover letter helps the editor see the paper’s novelty and fit quickly.

If the journal permits it, authors may also suggest suitable reviewers or identify legitimate conflicts that make particular reviewers inappropriate.

24. Responding to reviewers: treat revision as part of the research process

Peer review is not merely an obstacle between submission and publication.

Good reviewer comments often reveal places where the research highway is incomplete or difficult to follow.

A response should therefore be systematic.

For each comment:

Reviewer comment
→ response
→ change made
→ exact location in revised manuscript

If you agree with the reviewer, make the change clearly.

If you disagree, explain the technical reason respectfully and provide evidence.

Do not answer criticism emotionally.

The objective is not to defeat the reviewer.

The objective is to make the scientific record stronger and to show the editor that every concern has been considered carefully.

A strong revision often improves not only language but also the logic and completeness of the paper.

25. Publication carries continuing responsibility

Publication is not the end of responsibility for the work.

Authors remain responsible for the integrity of the published record.

Before signing publication agreements, understand the journal’s policies concerning:

  • copyright,
  • reuse of figures and tables,
  • accepted manuscripts,
  • repositories,
  • open access,
  • and later reuse in books, lectures, or reviews.

Publication policies have changed substantially over time and differ among publishers, so the current agreement—not habit—should govern what may be reused.

The CCC rule revisited

After all the individual sections are written, return to the three questions.

Creativity

Is there something new?

Can I state it in one or two sentences?

Can the reader find it in the title, abstract, introduction, discussion, and conclusions?

Consistency

Does the whole paper tell the same story?

Do the symbols, data, terminology, figures, arguments, and conclusions agree with each other?

Does each section contribute to the question established at the beginning?

Completeness

Can the reader travel from the question to the conclusion without encountering a missing section of road?

Have I provided every assumption, method, piece of evidence, and argument that is essential?

Have I omitted only information that is unnecessary to the main story?

These three requirements reinforce one another.

A paper with creativity but no consistency feels fragmented.

A paper with consistency but no creativity may be well organized but unimportant.

A paper with creativity and consistency but missing critical evidence is incomplete.

That is why I treat the three together.

A practical writing sequence

For an engineering paper, the full process can be summarized as:

1. Ask whether there is something NEW
2. Define the research question
3. Identify the evidence needed to answer it
4. Build the outline
5. Prepare the key figures and tables
6. Write the methods so the evidence is reproducible
7. Present the important results
8. Explain why the results make engineering sense
9. State the conclusions supported by those results
10. Write/refine the introduction around the actual contribution
11. Condense the story into the abstract
12. Refine the title
13. Check CCC across the complete manuscript
14. Adapt to the target journal
15. Submit with a focused cover letter
16. Respond systematically to peer review

The exact order of drafting can vary.

Many researchers do not literally write Title → Abstract → Introduction → Methods in that sequence.

What matters is that the finished paper forms one coherent path.

Engineering takeaway

The goal of scientific writing is not to make the paper sound complicated.

It is to make the research reasoning transferable from one mind to another.

The highway analogy therefore remains useful:

Creativity gives us somewhere worth going.
Consistency keeps the road headed toward the same destination.
Completeness makes sure there are no missing sections of the highway.

Or, more compactly:

A good research paper contains a new destination, one continuous road, and no missing bridge.

That is what I mean by the CCC rule.
